## Project Description

In many real-world applications of AI, the set of interacting agents operates within large collectives where centralized coordination is infeasible. Robots navigating crowds, employees collaborating in organizations, or defenders securing networks must learn optimal behaviors without sharing private information or relying on a central trainer. We call these use-inspired many-agent systems, and their decentralized, partially observable, and mixed-motive nature necessitates new capabilities and modeling for decision making compared to standard cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning.

In this research, we address the challenges to multi-agent decision making caused by three core complexities:

1. **collective scale**, where the joint decision space grows exponentially with the number of agents. This could include modeling population behaviors using statistical mechanics approximations rather than explicit joint actions, or leveraging graph neural networks to exploit sparse local interactions where agents only observe their immediate neighbors.

2. **mixed-motive learning**, where agents simultaneously cooperate for shared goals and compete for private benefits. This could include employees cooperating to achieve team milestones while competing for a fixed bonus pool, requiring new representations that handle the tension between group optimality and individual self-interest without a central critic.

3. **type dynamism**, where the capabilities and attributes of agents change over time due to internal evolution or external events. This could include agents learning new skills through training or modifying their objectives due to role shifts, necessitating the modeling of type morphisms to prevent learned behaviors from becoming suboptimal as the environment evolves.
